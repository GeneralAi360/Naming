# Published expert principles used by the skill

## Purpose and boundary

This file translates publicly documented naming approaches into operational lenses for the skill. It does not claim that any named practitioner or agency endorses this repository, participated in a council, or would reach the same conclusion on a specific project.

The experts below are not presented as an objective global ranking. They are included because their published work contributes materially different, complementary methods: strategic process, competitive taxonomy, phonetics, memorability, creative divergence, linguistic/cultural screening, and professional evaluation.

The controller must preserve disagreement between schools instead of blending them into vague “best practice.”

---

## 1. David Placek / Lexicon Branding lens

### Published emphasis

Lexicon’s public materials emphasize that a name is a durable strategic asset, not a decorative label. Its current published process gives unusual weight to:

- broad quantity before convergence;
- sound symbolism and letter/syllable structure;
- processing fluency;
- attention plus surprise;
- small creative teams and a separate linguistic/engineering layer;
- names that feel both new and cognitively accessible;
- avoiding the demand that a six-to-eight-letter name tell the company’s entire story.

Lexicon describes strong names as creating a strategic advantage by getting attention, holding attention, and introducing surprise. Its “surprisingly familiar” idea is especially useful: a candidate should often have enough familiar structure to be processed, plus enough unexpectedness to be distinctive.

### What the skill adopts

The `PHONETIC_AND_SOUND_SYMBOLISM_LINGUIST` must assess:

1. phonemic character independently of dictionary meaning;
2. stress, cadence, consonant/vowel energy, and repetition;
3. processing fluency without equating fluency with blandness;
4. the balance of familiarity and surprise;
5. whether the name creates an ownable sonic pattern;
6. whether the name is a useful building block for the story rather than an overloaded description.

The generation engine must include phonetic-first synthesis and broad private generation before final selection.

### What the skill must not overclaim

- Sound symbolism can influence perception; it does not deterministically predict purchase behavior.
- Individual letters do not carry one universal meaning in every language and context.
- A compelling sound does not override a direct legal conflict or severe language risk.

### Council question

> Does this name attract and hold attention through sound, fluency, and controlled surprise—and can its sonic structure compound with repeated use?

## 2. Rob Meyerson / How Brands Are Built lens

### Published emphasis

Rob Meyerson’s professional naming framework treats naming as a disciplined sequence rather than a brainstorm:

- create and approve a naming brief;
- define approaches and constructs;
- generate a broad candidate universe;
- shortlist against the brief;
- prescreen trademarks;
- examine linguistics and domains;
- present and decide with structured rationale.

The brief aligns decision-makers before the subjective part begins. Meyerson also separates **naming approach** (how directly the name relates to meaning) from **name construct** (how the name is formed), which prevents teams from treating all invented or compound names as strategically identical.

### What the skill adopts

The `NAMING_STRATEGY_LEAD` owns:

1. a signed-off brief before full generation;
2. separation of strategic approach and linguistic construction;
3. explicit decision authority and veto criteria;
4. traceability from brief criterion to shortlist rationale;
5. staged shortlisting and screening;
6. a presentation that explains candidates in context rather than as an unstructured word list.

### What the skill must not overclaim

- A formal process cannot eliminate subjective judgment.
- Stakeholder alignment is not permission to flatten all distinctiveness.
- Screening is not legal clearance.

### Council question

> Is this candidate a strategically optimal answer to the approved brief, and can every important recommendation be traced to an agreed criterion?

## 3. Alexandra Watkins / Eat My Words lens

### Published emphasis

Alexandra Watkins is known for evaluating names through the SMILE & SCRATCH framework.

`SMILE` asks whether a name is:

- **Suggestive** — evokes rather than merely describes;
- **Meaningful** — connects to the brand or audience;
- **Imagery-rich** — creates pictures and associations;
- **Legs** — supports wordplay, campaigns, product language, and verbal identity;
- **Emotional** — creates a feeling rather than indifference.

`SCRATCH` flags names that are:

- **Spelling-challenged**;
- **Copycat**;
- **Restrictive**;
- **Annoying**;
- **Tame**;
- dependent on the **Curse of knowledge**;
- **Hard to pronounce**.

Her public materials emphasize customer attraction, ease of use, emotional connection, and names that give marketers useful verbal material.

### What the skill adopts

The `VERBAL_IDENTITY_AND_MEMORABILITY_CRITIC` must:

1. run SMILE/SCRATCH as a practical filter;
2. examine whether the name creates imagery and emotional response;
3. test whether it has “legs” for descriptors, campaign language, feature names, onboarding language, and social conversation;
4. penalize insider-only meanings and explanation dependence;
5. distinguish a healthy puzzle from confusing spelling or pronunciation.

### What the skill must not overclaim

- Playfulness is not required for every market.
- A serious enterprise or regulated brand may prioritize trust and precision over overt wordplay.
- A strong SMILE profile does not substitute for competitive or legal screening.

### Council question

> Will the audience remember, repeat, and build language around this name—or will they scratch their heads, misspell it, or need an insider explanation?

## 4. Igor International lens

### Published emphasis

Igor’s public naming guide and taxonomy emphasize competitive separation and engagement. Names are plotted against category competitors rather than judged in isolation. The agency distinguishes functional/descriptive, invented, experiential, and evocative territories and examines how much meaning, imagery, story, tension, and audience participation a name offers.

Igor also argues against “literal objection” as the only decision method. A provocative name may contain tension or a negative association yet still create attention and engagement when it maps well to positioning. Its critique of bland consensus is useful: teams often remove every edge until the result is generic and forgettable.

### What the skill adopts

The `COMPETITIVE_NAMING_CARTOGRAPHER` must:

1. plot competitors by semantic approach and construction;
2. identify clusters, clichés, suffix trends, metaphor saturation, and low-engagement “wallflowers”;
3. locate white space before generation;
4. assess `ENGAGEMENT_MATERIAL`: imagery, associations, story, tension, cultural material, and audience participation;
5. classify objections as strategic, material, idiosyncratic, or merely literal deconstruction.

### What the skill must not overclaim

- Provocation is not automatically good.
- Negative tension must not create material harm, exclusion, regulatory problems, or severe cross-cultural risk.
- “Interesting” is necessary in many categories but not sufficient for trust, usability, or clearance.

### Council question

> Does the name occupy an engaging and differentiated position in the actual naming landscape, or is it another category-shaped wallflower?

## 5. Eli Altman / A Hundred Monkeys lens

### Published emphasis

Eli Altman presents naming as systematic creative work, not a mystical eureka event. Useful published ideas include:

- begin with positioning;
- deliberately generate bad names to lower pressure and expose anti-criteria;
- mix individual and group work;
- combine offline and online research;
- avoid over-description;
- earn stakeholder buy-in to the process before asking for buy-in to a result;
- take enough risk to create personality and memorability.

The “bad names” exercise is especially valuable as reverse engineering: identifying why names fail creates an `ANTI_NAME_PROFILE` that can be inverted into useful criteria.

### What the skill adopts

The `CREATIVE_NAMING_DIRECTOR` must:

1. reject waiting for one magical insight;
2. use systematic waves and deliberate constraints;
3. employ the bad-name/reverse-brainstorm exercise when the team is blocked;
4. combine solitary generation, group collision, research, and physical/offline prompts where possible;
5. protect distinctive ideas from premature consensus;
6. insist that creative risk remains connected to positioning.

### What the skill must not overclaim

- Difficulty of spelling or pronunciation is not intrinsically memorable.
- Risk is useful only when the audience can still adopt and repeat the name.
- Stakeholder process does not replace market, language, domain, or legal evidence.

### Council question

> Is this candidate the result of a genuine creative search with enough nerve and personality, or did the team select the easiest name to agree on?

### Primary/public sources

- A Hundred Monkeys, “Don’t Call It That: A Naming Workbook”

## 6. Catchword lens

### Published emphasis

Catchword’s public process treats names as short stories and mental shorthand for the audience’s experience. Its methodology combines:

- brand definition and naming strategy;
- candidate creation across name styles;
- a larger shortlist than the team emotionally wants, because legal screening removes many names;
- domain, trademark, and social screening;
- structured linguistic and cultural evaluation;
- native-speaker review in the target markets;
- careful use of audience research, because context-free preference often favors literal names.

Catchword explicitly distinguishes descriptive, suggestive, and “empty vessel”/abstract possibilities and notes the trade-off between immediate semantic clarity and legal ownability.

### What the skill adopts

The `CROSS_CULTURAL_LINGUIST`, `DIGITAL_AVAILABILITY_RESEARCHER`, and `TRADEMARK_PRE_SCREENER` must:

1. keep multiple candidates alive until screening is sufficiently advanced;
2. separate domain, social, company, app, and trademark conflicts;
3. use native speakers and more than one respondent for material language decisions;
4. test pronunciation, spelling, meaning, brand fit, and similar existing brands;
5. treat consumer research as one signal, not a binding popularity vote;
6. examine future product and portfolio architecture.

### What the skill must not overclaim

- A database search is not comprehensive legal clearance.
- One respondent’s unusual association is not automatically a veto.
- A descriptive name may be appropriate in some contexts even if it is harder to protect.

### Council question

> Can this name survive real-world use across language, culture, portfolio, digital, and legal constraints without losing its strategic and emotional value?

## How the lenses intentionally disagree

The council should expect productive tension:

| Tension | Lens A | Lens B | Controller’s job |
|---|---|---|---|
| clarity vs ownability | descriptive immediacy | suggestive/evocative distance | determine how much meaning the name must carry versus the descriptor |
| fluency vs surprise | easy processing | stopping power and tension | find controlled surprise, not blandness or chaos |
| stakeholder comfort vs distinctiveness | process buy-in | resistance to bland consensus | distinguish material objections from preference anxiety |
| domain purity vs naming strength | exact `.com` convenience | strong name with alternate domain strategy | follow the approved domain policy, budget, and acquisition reality |
| literal audience preference vs long-term brand value | immediate comprehension | differentiated memory and extensibility | use observed behavior and context, not naked popularity |
| phonetic charisma vs legal risk | sonic pattern | likelihood of confusion | legal knockout wins; otherwise preserve the creative insight for a new construction |

The controller must never erase these tensions by averaging every opinion into a generic score.
