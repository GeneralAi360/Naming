# Creative generation system

## 1. Purpose

This document defines how the skill expands and constructs naming candidates without collapsing into predictable AI output. It separates three layers:

1. **Semantic source** — the territory, customer language, proprietary material, cultural code, or distant domain from which meaning comes.
2. **Meta-ideation method** — the technique used to escape the obvious search space.
3. **Naming construction method** — the way the final word, phrase, or structure is formed.

A strong wave intentionally combines all three. Example:

```text
semantic source: navigation + customer phrase “I don’t know the next step”
meta-method: focal objects
construction: semantic compression + coined blend
phonetic code: two syllables, firm opening, open vowel, clean ending
```

Do not mistake a list of suffixes for a creative system.

### 2.1 Method roles

- `PRIMARY` — strategically suited; receives the largest candidate budget.
- `SECONDARY` — provides breadth and alternative constructions.
- `EXPERIMENTAL` — deliberately tests the edge; kept separate until reviewed.
- `EXCLUDED` — conflicts with category, tone, regulation, language, or architecture.

### 2.2 Wave discipline

Every wave must state what is new relative to prior waves. A new wave is invalid if it merely changes one letter, repeats the same suffix, or stays inside the same semantic world.

# Part A — 18 classic naming construction methods

## 1. Word formation

### Mechanism

Create names using productive prefixes, suffixes, compounding rules, clipping, derivation, and grammatical transformation from real language.

### Procedure

1. Select strategically relevant roots from approved word banks.
2. List legitimate derivational possibilities in target languages.
3. Test noun, verb, adjective, agent, place, process, and result forms.
4. Remove forms that sound generic, awkward, or falsely etymological.
5. Apply phonetic code and collision checks.

### Good for

- names with a familiar linguistic anchor;
- category creation through a new but understandable term;
- products that may become verbs or roles.

### Failure modes

- mechanical suffix attachment;
- pseudo-Latin prestige;
- dictionary-correct but brand-dead forms;
- crowded `-ify`, `-ly`, `-io`, `-ex`, or fashionable endings.

---

## 2. Associations

### Mechanism

Generate from the web of ideas, emotions, situations, objects, people, actions, and sensory material surrounding the product and desired transformation.

### Procedure

1. Start from problem, mechanism, result, identity, ritual, and emotion.
2. Create first-, second-, and third-order associations.
3. Mark category clichés and remove them.
4. Move toward less obvious but still explainable associations.
5. Convert the best nodes using several construction methods.

### Good for

- suggestive and evocative names;
- human and emotionally resonant brands.

### Failure modes

- staying at first-order category words;
- confusing personal associations with audience-shared associations.

---

## 3. Combining words

### Mechanism

Join two or more meaningful units as compounds, blends, portmanteaux, or structured phrases.

### Procedure

1. Combine words from different banks, not two obvious category synonyms.
2. Test full compound, clipped compound, overlap blend, and phrase form.
3. Preserve pronounceability and avoid accidental spelling traps.
4. Check whether both components add meaning or one is decorative.

### Good for

- compressed explanations;
- “familiar + new” constructions;
- names with multiple associations.

### Failure modes

- `Smart + Category`, `Next + Category`, `Mind + Function` constructions;
- overlong or corporate-sounding stacks;
- copycat compounds.

---

## 4. Borrowing from other languages

### Mechanism

Use a real word or morpheme from another language when its sound, meaning, and cultural context fit the brand.

### Procedure

1. Choose languages relevant to market, founder story, product mechanism, or desired sound.
2. Verify real spelling, pronunciation, meaning, register, and inflection.
3. Check whether the word is current, archaic, sacred, offensive, gendered, or culturally sensitive.
4. Test usability for non-native speakers.
5. Label any free brand interpretation honestly.

### Good for

- heritage and geographic stories;
- globally portable sound with real meaning.

### Failure modes

- invented “translations”;
- decorative Latin/Greek/Sanskrit;
- appropriation or sacred terms;
- words impossible for primary users to pronounce.

---

## 5. Abstract names

### Mechanism

Select a real or coined name with little direct category meaning, allowing the brand to fill it over time.

### Procedure

1. Define the emotional and phonetic character first.
2. Select forms with strong distinctiveness and manageable adoption.
3. Build the descriptor and launch story needed to contextualize the name.
4. Check future scope and legal ownability.

### Good for

- master brands;
- broad future expansion;
- well-funded or highly distinctive launches.

### Failure modes

- random letters;
- no memory hook;
- expecting the audience to infer a complex story without support.

---

## 6. Neologism

### Mechanism

Create a new word through affixation, blending, clipping, recombination, phonetic invention, or abbreviation.

### Procedure

1. Decide whether the neologism is meaning-led, sound-led, or hybrid.
2. Use stable syllable patterns.
3. Check likely pronunciation and spelling variants.
4. Avoid being one edit away from an existing brand.
5. Provide honest construction, not invented ancient roots.

### Good for

- ownability;
- global products;
- category creation.

### Failure modes

- pharmaceutical sound by accident;
- interchangeable startup noise;
- forced explanation;
- unreadable consonant clusters.

---

## 7. Literature, legends, myths, and characters

### Mechanism

Use archetypes, stories, places, objects, or lesser-known motifs from literature, myth, and folklore.

### Procedure

1. Start from the archetypal function, not celebrity recognition alone.
2. Research rights, cultural context, religious sensitivity, and existing brands.
3. Prefer transformable motifs and indirect references over famous protected characters.
4. Verify whether the story supports the brand’s values over time.

### Good for

- rich story and imagery;
- archetypal brands;
- hospitality, culture, entertainment, and consumer products.

### Failure modes

- using a famous person/character as borrowed attention;
- legal or reputation risk;
- obscure reference requiring a lecture;
- cultural misuse.

---

## 8. Repetition of words or syllables

### Mechanism

Use reduplication, echo, mirrored syllables, or repeated sound patterns.

### Procedure

1. Test full repetition, partial echo, consonant echo, and vowel echo.
2. Align rhythm with desired energy.
3. Check whether the result feels playful, childish, premium, or technical.
4. Use only when that tone fits.

### Good for

- recall and oral transmission;
- playful consumer products;
- rhythmic global names.

### Failure modes

- infantilization;
- novelty without meaning;
- confusing near-repetition.

---

## 9. Rhyme and consonance

### Mechanism

Build names using rhyme, internal rhyme, assonance, consonance, or rhythmic parallelism.

### Procedure

1. Identify target phonetic pattern.
2. Generate real and coined rhyming pairs.
3. Remove sing-song forms that undermine trust.
4. Test recall and pronunciation in every target language.

### Good for

- campaigns, products, entertainment, and talkable brands.

### Failure modes

- jingle-like cheapness;
- forced grammar;
- culturally awkward rhyme.

---

## 10. Metaphor

### Mechanism

Name the brand through an image that embodies mechanism, benefit, identity, or transformation.

### Procedure

1. Define exactly what property transfers from metaphor to product.
2. Search near and distant metaphor domains.
3. Avoid category clichés.
4. Test whether the metaphor scales beyond the first feature.
5. Check conflicting existing brands in adjacent industries.

### Good for

- semantic compression;
- emotional and image-rich names;
- strong visual identity.

### Failure modes

- selecting a pretty object with no strategic link;
- overused navigation, light, bridge, orbit, or growth metaphors;
- overly literal visual identity.

---

## 11. Humor

### Mechanism

Use wit, incongruity, understatement, exaggeration, cultural observation, or wordplay.

### Procedure

1. Define whether the humor is warm, dry, absurd, rebellious, self-aware, or satirical.
2. Ensure the target audience—not only the creator—understands it.
3. Test repetition fatigue and seriousness requirements.
4. Keep legal, cultural, and platform risks separate.

### Good for

- challenger brands;
- hospitality, media, creators, entertainment, consumer products.

### Failure modes

- joke with a short half-life;
- offensive shock replacing strategy;
- a name stakeholders are embarrassed to recommend.

---

## 12. Phrase describing use or result

### Mechanism

Use a short sentence, instruction, promise, ritual, or conversational phrase.

### Procedure

1. Extract real customer phrases.
2. Identify action/result phrases that are natural in speech.
3. Compress where necessary without losing rhythm.
4. Test trademark distinctiveness and domain practicality.

### Good for

- services, communities, media, campaigns, lifestyle brands;
- high talkability and customer-language fit.

### Failure modes

- generic claims;
- excessive length;
- difficult internationalization;
- slogan mistaken for a master brand.

---

## 13. Contrast

### Mechanism

Combine opposites or position the name against category expectations.

### Procedure

1. Define the real contradiction in customer experience or market belief.
2. Generate semantic, emotional, temporal, scale, or status opposites.
3. Ensure tension is productive and not merely confusing.
4. Connect the contrast to the paradigm shift.

### Good for

- challenger brands;
- new categories;
- strong memorability.

### Failure modes

- clever contradiction with no product truth;
- negative meaning that dominates;
- confusing regulated audiences.

---

## 14. Transliteration

### Mechanism

Represent a word or phrase from one writing system in another.

### Procedure

1. Select a real culturally relevant source.
2. Map likely transliterations and pronunciation variants.
3. Choose a form that target users can read and type.
4. Check meanings in both scripts and existing marks.

### Good for

- local-to-global brands;
- heritage stories;
- distinctive sound.

### Failure modes

- multiple uncontrollable spellings;
- loss of meaning;
- exoticism without authenticity.

---

## 15. Superiority

### Mechanism

Signal leadership, scale, authority, completeness, firstness, or ultimate performance.

### Procedure

1. Determine whether the claim is legally and factually supportable.
2. Prefer symbolic or tonal superiority over unsupported absolute claims.
3. Test whether the name feels confident or insecure.

### Good for

- performance-driven products;
- premium or category-leader positioning.

### Failure modes

- generic `Pro`, `Max`, `Ultra`, `No.1` constructions;
- regulatory or substantiation problems;
- arrogance without proof.

---

## 16. Alliteration

### Mechanism

Repeat initial or internal consonant sounds across a phrase or compound.

### Procedure

1. Choose a consonant aligned with the phonetic code.
2. Build natural, meaningful combinations.
3. Test tongue-twister risk.
4. Check whether the repetition improves recall or feels gimmicky.

### Good for

- phrase names;
- oral recall;
- editorial and consumer brands.

### Failure modes

- forced vocabulary;
- pronunciation problems;
- childish tone.

---

## 17. Onomatopoeia

### Mechanism

Use or invent a form that imitates sound, motion, tactile experience, or product action.

### Procedure

1. Identify real sensory action.
2. List culture-specific sound representations.
3. Build a pronounceable form aligned with product benefit.
4. Test cross-language interpretation.

### Good for

- physical products;
- motion, audio, food, cleaning, tools, children’s products.

### Failure modes

- sound means something different across languages;
- overly childish result;
- weak connection to a non-sensory service.

---

## 18. Supercreative / provocative construction

### Mechanism

Use deliberate incongruity, irony, challenge, absurdity, cultural tension, or unconventional phrase structure.

### Procedure

1. Define the strategic boundary being challenged.
2. Choose the type of provocation: wit, taboo inversion, absurdity, blunt truth, anti-category, social irony, or unexpected scale.
3. Generate separately from the standard pool.
4. Run harm, culture, legal, stakeholder, and longevity checks.
5. Require a stronger success thesis than for conventional names.

### Good for

- challenger brands;
- culture-led products;
- youth, entertainment, creator, hospitality, and campaign naming.

### Failure modes

- shock without relevance;
- slurs, stereotypes, or borrowed celebrity attention;
- trend decay;
- stakeholder inability to use the name publicly.

# Part B — 18 advanced naming construction methods

## 19. Category escape

### Mechanism

Deliberately leave the category’s obvious vocabulary and borrow from a distant semantic world.

### Procedure

1. Map category clichés.
2. Select distant worlds with analogous mechanisms or emotions.
3. Identify transferable properties.
4. Create names that gain new meaning in the product context.
5. Validate that the leap is intriguing, not arbitrary.

### Success signal

The name feels fresh in the category while becoming understandable after a short descriptor.

---

## 20. Semantic compression

### Mechanism

Compress a complex product truth into one image, action, object, or short construction.

### Procedure

1. Write the product mechanism in a full sentence.
2. Reduce it to a causal verb.
3. Reduce the desired transformation to an image.
4. Search for a single symbol carrying both.
5. Test whether the compressed idea still scales.

### Failure mode

A vague metaphor that needs a long pitch to appear relevant.

---

## 21. Proprietary fragment

### Mechanism

Build from a unique internal term, method, founder phrase, data structure, ritual, or product mechanism.

### Procedure

1. Interview founders/team and inspect product language.
2. Identify terms competitors cannot authentically claim.
3. Extract fragments with phonetic potential.
4. transform them through several construction methods.
5. protect confidential material and verify rights.

### Advantage

Higher authenticity and lower probability of generic AI sameness.

---

## 22. Distant-domain collision

### Mechanism

Combine two remote semantic worlds to create a novel conceptual field.

### Procedure

1. Choose domain A from product truth.
2. Choose domain B from a distant world.
3. list shared structures, tensions, verbs, and objects.
4. generate at the intersection, not by merely concatenating words.
5. test explainability and cultural coherence.

### Example pattern

`behavioral insight × cartography` can yield territories around coordinates, bearings, contour, signal, and routes—not simply “MindMap.”

---

## 23. Phonetic-first synthesis

### Mechanism

Design the sound pattern before attaching meaning.

### Procedure

1. Define emotional sound attributes.
2. create syllable frames.
3. generate pronounceable forms.
4. eliminate conflict and undesirable associations.
5. attach only honest brand interpretations.

### Advantage

Can produce highly distinctive and memorable invented names.

### Risk

Retrofitted fake etymology or startup-noise sameness.

---

## 24. Deep morpheme mutation

### Mechanism

Transform roots structurally rather than replacing one letter.

### Procedure

- change syllable boundary;
- alter stress;
- blend non-adjacent fragments;
- translate concept before recombination;
- change grammatical category;
- invert or mirror sound pattern;
- preserve only a property, not the visible root.

### Knockout

If an ordinary listener would call it a misspelling of an existing brand, reject.

---

## 25. Phrase compression

### Mechanism

Turn a customer phrase, promise, ritual, or story into a compact brand form.

### Procedure

1. collect verbatim customer language;
2. identify emotionally loaded phrases;
3. remove function words;
4. test clipping, acronym, blend, and rhythmic abbreviation;
5. retain recognizability without awkward shorthand.

---

## 26. Verbable naming

### Mechanism

Create a name that can naturally become a verb or action in user speech.

### Test

- “Send it through ___.”
- “We ___ed the file.”
- “Let’s ___ this.”

### Risk

Do not sacrifice distinctiveness merely for grammatical convenience. Consider genericide and portfolio implications.

---

## 27. Ritual/action naming

### Mechanism

Name the repeated user behavior rather than the product object.

### Procedure

1. map the user journey;
2. identify high-value recurring moments;
3. capture the action, transition, or ritual;
4. build names around that behavioral unit.

### Advantage

The brand can become embedded in habit and usage language.

---

## 28. Contrarian naming

### Mechanism

Take a credible position opposite to a category cliché.

### Procedure

1. state the dominant belief;
2. identify what the product proves differently;
3. define the emotional contrast;
4. build names from the new belief.

### Risk

Contrarianism without product truth becomes empty rebellion.

---

## 29. Enemy-based naming

### Mechanism

Name the friction, waste, opacity, anxiety, or broken norm the product eliminates.

### Forms

- direct enemy;
- absence of enemy;
- escape from enemy;
- tool against enemy;
- world after enemy.

### Risk

Avoid turning a temporary problem into the permanent identity of the brand or attacking a vulnerable group.

---

## 30. Founder/heritage code

### Mechanism

Encode founder history, surname fragments, geography, dates, craft, family symbols, language, or origin principles.

### Procedure

1. separate meaningful heritage from vanity;
2. identify transferable symbols;
3. check scalability beyond the founder;
4. examine pronunciation and cultural interpretation.

---

## 31. Visual-letter naming

### Mechanism

Consider letterform, symmetry, ligature, monogram, negative space, and wordmark rhythm during naming—without letting visual cleverness dominate usability.

### Procedure

1. identify distinctive letters permitted by phonetic code;
2. test lowercase/uppercase silhouettes;
3. test favicon/monogram opportunities;
4. ensure the spoken form remains strong.

### Boundary

This is a naming criterion, not a logo-design phase.

---

## 32. Domain-led construction

### Mechanism

Design natural, slightly longer constructions to improve the chance of workable domains.

### Procedure

1. define approved TLD and modifier policy;
2. prefer 7–12 letter forms or natural two-word compounds when exact short `.com` is unrealistic;
3. keep naming quality primary;
4. screen domains before emotional attachment.

### Risk

A free domain does not rescue a weak name.

---

## 33. Bilingual resonance

### Mechanism

Build a form that is pronounceable and positively suggestive in two or more target languages.

### Procedure

1. define acceptable pronunciation variance;
2. test shared phoneme inventory;
3. examine meanings and morphology in both languages;
4. validate with native speakers.

### Risk

False friends, accidental slang, and multiple spellings.

---

## 34. Hidden double meaning

### Mechanism

Create a primary intuitive reading plus a secondary rewarding interpretation.

### Rule

The name must work before the explanation. The hidden layer is a bonus, not life support.

### Risk

Over-engineered puzzles and fake origin stories.

---

## 35. Controlled irregularity

### Mechanism

Introduce one deliberate unusual feature in spelling, rhythm, spacing, or grammar while preserving adoption.

### Procedure

1. make the normal form explicit;
2. change one element only;
3. test whether people can still pronounce, remember, and type it;
4. reject if the irregularity looks like an error.

### Advantage

Distinctiveness without complete unfamiliarity.

---

## 36. User-language extraction

### Mechanism

Derive names from the audience’s real words, inner monologue, recurring phrases, complaints, desired identity, and rituals.

### Procedure

1. source actual interviews, reviews, transcripts, support logs, search queries, or supplied audience analysis;
2. separate widespread language from one-off quotes;
3. identify emotionally compressed phrases;
4. transform them through phrase compression, metaphor, or word formation;
5. preserve tone without exploiting fear or stigma.

### Advantage

High recognition and reduced marketer-language distance.

# Part C — Meta-ideation methods

These methods do not directly define the final word. They change the problem space from which names are built.

## 1. Morphological box

### Purpose

Systematically combine independent dimensions instead of combining random words.

### Naming adaptation

Create columns such as:

| Dimension | Examples |
|---|---|
| customer transformation | clarity, control, relief, status, belonging |
| mechanism | reveal, connect, filter, guide, translate |
| image world | optics, navigation, craft, music, ecology |
| emotional tone | calm, sharp, warm, rebellious, playful |
| construct | metaphor, compound, coined, phrase, real word |
| phonetic frame | CVCV, CVCVC, two-beat phrase, mirrored syllables |

Generate from unusual but coherent combinations. Do not exhaust every permutation mechanically.

---

## 2. Synectics

### Purpose

Make the familiar strange and the strange familiar through analogy.

### Naming adaptation

Use:

- direct analogies from nature, engineering, art, rituals, or society;
- personal analogy: “If the product were a living actor, what would it do?”;
- symbolic analogy: compress a contradiction into two words;
- fantasy analogy: imagine the impossible ideal mechanism.

Convert analogies into semantic territories, then apply naming construction methods.

---

## 3. Focal objects

### Purpose

Transfer properties of random objects to the naming problem.

### Naming adaptation

1. Choose 3–5 unrelated objects.
2. List their actions, textures, structures, failures, and cultural associations.
3. Transfer only properties that reveal a real product truth.
4. Create territories and candidates from the strongest transfers.

The method is invalid if the random object remains decorative.

---

## 4. SCAMPER

Apply to an existing territory, candidate family, or category language:

- **Substitute** — replace obvious category root, metaphor, or language.
- **Combine** — merge distant mechanisms or emotions.
- **Adapt** — borrow a structure from another category.
- **Modify / Magnify / Minify** — change scale, tempo, intensity, or syllabic shape.
- **Put to another use** — repurpose a customer phrase, technical term, or ritual.
- **Eliminate** — remove expected category signals, vowels, suffixes, or descriptor burden.
- **Reverse / Rearrange** — invert belief, word order, sound sequence, or perspective.

Use SCAMPER to escape a stuck family, not to make endless trivial variations.

---

## 5. Six Thinking Hats

Use as a review/generation sequence, not as fictional personalities:

- **White** — facts, source words, market evidence, constraints.
- **Red** — immediate emotion and intuition, clearly labeled.
- **Black** — risks, conflicts, misuse, negative associations.
- **Yellow** — upside, story, memorability, activation potential.
- **Green** — alternative territories and constructions.
- **Blue** — controller synthesis and next experiment.

This method is useful before the formal expert council or when stakeholders are stuck.

---

## 6. TRIZ-inspired contradiction solving

### Purpose

Use the desired contradiction as a source of naming advantage.

### Naming adaptation

1. State the contradiction: “The name must feel technically credible but human,” “global but rooted,” “simple but ownable.”
2. Identify which dimension can be separated: time, context, language, descriptor, sound, or architecture.
3. Create constructions that satisfy each side through different layers.

Example:

- abstract master name for ownability;
- clear descriptor for immediate category understanding.

Do not pretend this is a full formal TRIZ analysis unless formal TRIZ tools are actually used.

---

## 7. Walt Disney method

Use three sequential modes:

- **Dreamer** — unconstrained worlds, metaphors, category shifts, impossible names.
- **Realist** — pronunciation, audience, architecture, domain, launch context.
- **Critic** — failure conditions, confusion, collisions, durability.

Do not let the Critic enter during the first divergent pass. Do not let the Dreamer certify feasibility.

---

## 8. Reverse brainstorming / bad-name exercise

### Purpose

Expose anti-criteria and reduce pressure.

### Procedure

1. Deliberately create the worst plausible names.
2. Classify why each is bad.
3. Build `ANTI_NAME_PROFILE`.
4. Invert the failures into generation constraints.
5. Create a new wave.

Typical anti-patterns:

- copy the category;
- impossible spelling;
- accidental medical sound;
- trendy suffix;
- narrow feature lock-in;
- founder-insider joke;
- fake foreign prestige;
- domain-first nonsense.

---

## 9. Three chests

The user-supplied methodology named this method without explaining it. External descriptions commonly frame it as a decision-risk exercise:

1. first chest — possible negative consequences;
2. second chest — which risks are real/material;
3. third chest — antidotes or mitigations.

### Naming adaptation

For a bold candidate:

- Chest 1: list every plausible objection and failure.
- Chest 2: classify audience relevance, probability, and severity.
- Chest 3: identify mitigation through descriptor, spelling, launch story, domain strategy, or rejection.

Do not use it as a substitute for legal or native-speaker screening.

---

## 10. Robinson Crusoe resource method

External descriptions of this TRIZ/creative-imagination technique emphasize extreme resource constraints and discovering hidden uses of available objects.

### Naming adaptation

Imagine the project has:

- no access to category keywords;
- no trendy suffixes;
- no short `.com` assumption;
- no invented Latin;
- only customer language, founder history, product actions, and one distant domain.

Then identify hidden naming resources already present in the product, audience, workflow, geography, and culture.

This method is particularly useful when all obvious roots are occupied.

# Part D — Category creation mode

Use when the product introduces a meaningfully new mechanism, the current category is strategically limiting, or category language is too saturated.

## Step 1. Define the enemy

Document:

- what is outdated;
- what promise has become empty;
- what behavior or model should disappear;
- what the audience tolerates but resents;
- what the new brand refuses to normalize.

## Step 2. Define the paradigm shift

```text
FROM: old category logic
TO: new category logic
WHY NOW: market, technology, behavior, or cultural change
PROOF: product mechanism or evidence
```

## Step 3. Generate category-name structures

- verb + mission;
- profession + differentiating role;
- concept + persona;
- new compound;
- coined category term;
- mechanism + outcome;
- enemy-free state;
- ritual/action category.

Generate at least five structurally different category-name candidates.

## Step 4. Define category rules

- what becomes normal;
- what is forbidden;
- what terminology the category uses;
- what metrics define success;
- which products belong or do not belong.

## Step 5. Test category credibility

A category name fails when it is merely a slogan, has no product mechanism behind it, cannot be taught in one minute, or exists only to avoid competing honestly.

## Step 6. Decide architecture

Determine whether the category term is:

- generic market language;
- proprietary branded category;
- descriptor under the company name;
- thought-leadership phrase;
- temporary campaign frame.

Do not automatically try to trademark generic category language as a product name.

# Part E — Bold, memetic, and culturally disruptive naming prompts

These are opt-in experimental prompts derived from the user-supplied creative methodology. They are not appropriate for every project.

## Prompt families

1. **Hyperbole and pain** — exaggerate a real frustration until it becomes a vivid phrase.
2. **Simulated benefit** — name the feeling of already having achieved the result.
3. **Nobody would guess** — collide the category with an unexpected object or role.
4. **Missing everyday service** — name an unspoken ritual or recurring “life gap.”
5. **Expert in armor** — combine credibility with protective or rebellious imagery.
6. **Absurd cultural ritual** — transform a familiar tradition into an unexpected brand artifact.
7. **Only for a sharply defined audience** — identity-based in-group phrase without demeaning outsiders.
8. **Absurdly useful** — comic form with genuine functional truth.
9. **Therapeutically painful** — acknowledge an uncomfortable truth with care, not stigma.
10. **Office/home absurdity** — recognizable everyday chaos as naming material.
11. **Social irony** — reveal a contradiction in behavior or culture.
12. **Opposites collision** — combine incompatible tones, scales, or roles.
13. **Event/trend response** — only for campaign, drop, or time-limited product naming unless durability is proven.
14. **Cultural symptom** — name the deeper behavior behind a trend rather than the trend itself.
15. **Pseudo-scientific parody** — use only where audiences understand the satire and regulation is not implicated.

## IP and reputation constraints

Do not build a candidate around a living celebrity, famous character, franchise, protected brand, political figure, or public persona merely to borrow recognition. Such references may be used as a creative stimulus only; final candidates require independent original construction and rights review.

Do not use slurs, protected-class stereotypes, humiliating labels, or shock that creates material harm. “Provocative” means strategically tension-rich, not abusive.

## Shareability tests

For relevant consumer/cultural projects:

- `TALKABILITY` — does the name create a reason to mention it?
- `SCREENSHOTABILITY` — would the name itself be worth sharing?
- `QUOTEABILITY` — can it enter a sentence naturally?
- `MERCH_TEST` — would people voluntarily wear or display it?
- `HALF_LIFE_TEST` — will it survive after the current meme fades?

Weight these tests lightly for enterprise and regulated brands unless the brief explicitly prioritizes cultural disruption.

# Part F — New-wave protocol for “MORE / ЕЩЁ”

## 1. Diagnose before generating

Classify the previous failure:

- semantic sameness;
- phonetic sameness;
- construction sameness;
- insufficient proprietary material;
- overly category-shaped names;
- excessive abstraction;
- wrong creative intensity;
- domain scarcity;
- legal crowding;
- cultural mismatch;
- misunderstood user preference.

## 2. Update state

- add rejects and near forms to `NO_GO_REGISTER`;
- add exhausted roots/suffixes/frames to saturation lists;
- rewrite at least three generation dimensions;
- select 3–5 new territories or meta-methods;

- semantic territory;
- construction method;
- phonetic frame;
- creative intensity;
- word source;
- name length/form.

# Part G — Stopping conditions

Stop divergent generation when:

- all primary territories and methods have sufficient coverage;
- new candidates are mainly weaker duplicates;
- enough plausible names remain to survive screening;
- further generation would be less valuable than live collision research or user testing.

Restart only when downstream screening reveals a specific gap.
